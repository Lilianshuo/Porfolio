Case study 03 · Alibaba.com internship · behavioural-data research

Is a child’s age a useful segmentation signal?

Taobao’s maternal and infant category faces competition from content platforms. I tested whether a child’s age can segment shoppers, how far purchase behaviour can stand in for missing age data, and what the answer means for recommendations and page design.

Research questionCan a child’s age serve as a segmentation signal for maternal and infant shoppers, and what does it add to recommendations and page design?
My roleData cleaning and exploration; an age-classification experiment; a rule-based Top-K recommendation prototype.
Data and methods29,971 transaction records (955 with a child-age label). Buyer-penetration analysis; classifiers against a dummy baseline; offline Top-K evaluation.
Core findingsCategory preferences differ by age band. Age labels cover 3.2% of records. Purchase behaviour is a weak predictor of age. In an offline test, age-based lists had a higher hit rate at Top-20 but not at Top-5 or Top-10.

01Context and decision

Content platforms are drawing parents away from Taobao’s maternal and infant category. The team wanted to know whether a child’s age can be used to segment users, then to improve recommendations and the information experience. The decision at stake was how much product effort to put behind age-based personalisation.

Scope

This is a behavioural-data study. I did not interview or test with parents, so what parents care about is framed as hypotheses to test next.

02Data and constraints

The cleaned dataset linked child records to transactions by user ID. Only a small share could be matched to a usable child age.

Share of records with a usable child-age label

29,971 transaction records · 29,944 users

Why it matters: any age-based feature only reaches users who have a child profile. The 955 labelled rows exclude one anomalous record (age 32); the data-cleaning report counts 956.

Age-labelled records by child age band

n = 955 records · counts

The labelled sample is concentrated in ages 1–6. The 0–1 and 12–18 bands are too small to support firm conclusions, so I merged bands for modelling.

03Choosing the metric

My first plan was to compare purchase volume by age group. Exploring the data showed why that would mislead: most transactions have a quantity of 1, but 65 records have a quantity of 100 or more, and a purchase peak in November 2022 was more likely driven by a few large purchases than by broad demand. I switched to buyer penetration: unique buyers of a category divided by the age group’s unique users. It answers “how many people at this stage need this category”, not “how much did this stage buy”.

What the descriptive analysis showed

Category preferences differed across age groups, with some categories common to all groups and others specific to a stage. Differences between boys and girls looked smaller in this subsample, and the time trends did not diverge clearly by age group. This is a descriptive pattern in a 955-record subsample, not evidence that age drives purchasing.

04Can purchase behaviour reveal age?

When age is missing, could purchase behaviour alone indicate the child’s stage? I merged ages into three bands (0–3, 3–6, 6–18) and used category, gender, quantity and purchase timing. I built a “most frequent class” dummy baseline first, so a single accuracy figure could not impress me, then compared three models on a user-level stratified 80/20 split (test n = 191 users).

My read

With this sample and these features, transaction behaviour does not reliably reveal a child’s age, so I did not propose it as an age-prediction capability. Collecting or confirming age directly is the more dependable route.

05An age-based Top-K prototype

Separately from the classifier, I asked a simpler question: if a child’s age is known, does it help choose which categories to show? For each age band I ranked categories by buyer penetration using only the training users, then checked the held-out test users. A “hit” means at least one category the test user actually bought appears in the top K for their own age band. I compared this with a global-popularity list of the same length, built from the same training users.

Hit rate at K: age-based list vs global popularity

Offline evaluation · test n=191 users · one random split · bars start at 0

How to read the chart. The labels under each K are absolute differences in hit rate, in percentage points (at Top-20, 0.414 − 0.361 ≈ 5.3 points, roughly 10 of the 191 test users). The lists come from training users only, so no test purchases informed them, and the age-based list assumes the user’s age band is known. The data contain 261 category IDs, so a Top-30 list already covers a meaningful share of them.

What I took from it

Age-based recommendations showed a 5.3 percentage-point higher hit rate at Top-20 in this split, while the popularity baseline performed slightly better at Top-5 and Top-10. This suggests a possible benefit at broader recommendation coverage, but does not establish a consistent advantage. It is an offline hit-rate check on a single split of 191 users, assuming each user’s true age band is known; it is not a measure of clicks, conversion or revenue, which is why I kept the recommendation modest.

06From findings to recommendations

Age seems better at answering “what might this stage need” than “what does this person want now”. So I recommended it as a coarse auxiliary signal. The table below holds my design suggestions for the team; none has been launched or tested yet.

ObservationDesign suggestionPriority and effort (my assessment)
Age bands differ in preferred categoriesAge filters, age-stage entry points and tags in search and listingsShort term; low effort
Feedback labelled only as “Taobao buyer” is hard to judgeGroup reviews by child age or scenario, with a clear source and no invented group labelsShort term; low–medium effort
Some categories are common across agesA general “essentials” module alongside age-specific listsShort term
Parents may weigh material, safety and age-suitabilityRaise visibility of age range, safety and material informationMedium term; needs user research first
Bulk purchases distort sales rankingsSeparate bulk buying from broad demand; avoid ranking on sales aloneShort term

The page-level suggestions come from my observation of search and detail pages and a comparison with content platforms; they are hypotheses about parents’ decisions, to be tested with parents.

07Limits and next steps

  • The 955-record subsample may not represent all users; the 0–1 and 12–18 bands are very small.
  • Categories are IDs without names, which limited interpretation.
  • One random split with 191 test users; no repeated splits or confidence intervals.
  • No qualitative research. Whether parents want age-based information, and what they would trust, is unknown.

Next steps I proposed

  1. Interview parents about what drives choices in different child stages, and which age or safety information they trust.
  2. Usability-test age filters and age-grouped reviews on real pages.
  3. Run an A/B test only after those steps, with agreed outcome metrics; improve age-data coverage and check privacy transparency.
