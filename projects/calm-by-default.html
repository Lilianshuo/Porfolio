Case study 01 · Individual MSc project · 12 weeks

Calm by Default

Notifications keep students reachable and put them under pressure. Muting everything trades one problem for another, so I researched what students actually need to feel safe not checking, and designed a prototype around it.

01Why it matters: focus and connection

Students need to concentrate, and they also need to stay reachable. Constant cues invite checking and can affect focus (Gómez-Muñoz & Baelo, 2026; Veissière & Stendel, 2018), but muting everything trades one problem for another: more control, less connectedness. The project defines notification-related anxiety as momentary worry, pressure or unease arising from everyday notification interactions (adapted from Alutaybi et al., 2020).

Decision this research supports

What kind of notification intervention is worth designing for students: one that keeps connectedness and responsiveness, balances automation, setup effort and control, and limits checking-triggered phone use while supporting a return to the task.

Existing approaches and their trade-offs

Before designing, I reviewed existing approaches. Each one solves part of the problem and creates another:

Approach (source)Reported benefitReported trade-off
Disabling or muting notifications (Dekker et al., 2025)Improved perceived control and productivityDecreased connectedness, more workload and FOMO, anxiety; more phone pick-ups and longer screen time (Pielot & Rello, 2017; Liao & Sundar, 2022)
Greyscale display (Holte et al., 2023)Reduced anxietyDid not significantly change phone pick-ups
Batching three times a day (Fitz et al., 2019)Fewer unlocks, more controlEssential communication delayed and not contextualised
Boundary-based notification manager (Gross & Mueller, 2021)Separates study and personal spheresMay not suit students whose study–life boundaries blur
Sensor-inferred vs customisable DND (Qin et al., 2014; Jarupreechachan et al., 2023)Less manual effort, or more controlTrade-off between automation errors and configuration burden

02The result: Calm Profile (V2)

The research reframed the goal from “block more” to stay reachable without having to keep checking. Three design principles came out of it, and Calm Profile is the system-level mode built around them.

  1. Low-salience communication by default (based on Calm Technology)
    Reduce unnecessary salience; reassure importance with low-effort appraisal.
  2. Contextual, selective reachability with minimal effort
    Low-effort defaults, override and transparency, selective and contextual reachability.
  3. Bounded interaction and supported task resumption
    Bounded necessary interactions; support for returning to the task.

Calm Profile is a temporary, low-effort, system-level mode that preserves necessary communication while reducing notification salience, onward engagement and return difficulty. It differs from Do Not Disturb by supporting necessary communication without opening the full app. I chose a system-level mode because notifications come from many apps, so a single third-party app would struggle to manage them and would add configuration work.

  • Selective reachability. Suggested allowed apps (from survey data) and contacts and chats (from existing priority choices), always editable. Responds to findings 2 and 4.
  • Temporary customisation. “Adjust for now” changes apply until the mode is turned off. Responds to finding 2.
  • Bounded replies and “Review later”. Reply to a priority message in a focused window; save the rest without reply pressure. Responds to finding 3.

04Key findings and how they shaped the design

Mapping the whole journey showed that pressure can arise before checking and continue after the notification is addressed. That is why the design works at system level and across the journey, not only at the moment a notification arrives.

Explore the journey stage by stage

Choose a stage. Insights are my interpretation; the samples are small and exploratory, and a design response is a design decision, not a measured effect of one feature.

Stage 1 · Before the notification

Calm has to be easy to turn on, or setup becomes one more burden.

Observation · workshop (n=10)

Participants were immersed in an activity.

Pressure mechanism · my interpretation

Pressure has not started yet, so effort matters here: if turning on a calm mode needs setup, it adds the management burden people said they did not want (finding 4).

Evidence

Workshop journey maps (stage 1); finding 4 draws on synthesis across workshop and survey data.

Design response · Calm Profile

Low-effort activation. Calm Profile can be started from the Control Center shortcut, with low-effort defaults and minimal configuration.

Stage 2 · Notification arrives

Cues that stand out pull attention before anyone has chosen to look.

Observation · workshop (n=10)

Salient cues capture attention.

Pressure mechanism · my interpretation

Cues that stand out pull attention and prompt an importance judgement (finding 1).

Evidence

Workshop journey maps (stages 1–3).

Design response · Calm Profile

Low-salience by default. The design principle is low-salience communication, based on Calm Technology; the requirement was to reduce unnecessary notification salience.

Stage 3 · Immediate appraisal and feeling

Not knowing whether a message matters is what creates the urge to check.

Observation · workshop (n=10)

“Is this important?” Uncertainty creates checking pressure.

Pressure mechanism · my interpretation

Importance is contextual: staying undisturbed competes with staying reachable, so not knowing whether something matters creates pressure to check (findings 1 and 2).

Evidence

Workshop journey maps and discussion (stage 3). In the exploratory survey (n=31), messaging notifications were rated as both important and distracting.

Design response · Calm Profile

Selective reachability and temporary customisation. Suggested allowed apps (from survey data) and contacts (from existing priority choices), always editable; “Adjust for now” changes apply until the mode is turned off.

Stage 4 · Behaviour or response

A necessary check should not become a trip into the whole app.

Observation · workshop (n=10)

A necessary check can open the whole app.

Pressure mechanism · my interpretation

Checking one necessary message can lead into the full app and longer phone use (finding 3).

Evidence

Workshop stages 4–5. In the exploratory survey, 67.7% of the 31 respondents reported often continuing to use the phone longer than intended after checking.

Design response · Calm Profile

Bounded interaction. Reply to a priority message in a focused window instead of opening the full app.

Stage 5 · After-effects

The pressure outlasts the notification: getting back to the task is part of the problem.

Observation · workshop (n=10)

Digital drift and difficulty returning to the task.

Pressure mechanism · my interpretation

Pressure can continue after the notification is dealt with: extended use makes it harder to return to the earlier activity (finding 3).

Evidence

Workshop stages 4–5; the exploratory survey figure below (n=31).

Design response · Calm Profile

“Review later”. Save the rest of the messages without reply pressure, which supports returning to the task.

05Prototype evaluation, iterations and results

Six participants completed scenario-based tasks, followed by interviews and a survey. The test asked whether people could understand, start and control the mode: the questions a first prototype has to answer before its effect on anxiety or checking can be studied.

Concept understanding

Usability test, n=6

Usability findingWhat workedWhat did not
Learnable through useConcept understood through use; 6 of 6 rated concept understanding 4–5Onboarding caused initial confusion
Control vs configuration burdenEditable defaults reduced effortPreferences varied between manual and automated control
Activation and state clarityPost-activation use was generally easyActivation and state changes lacked salience
Bounded communicationReduced exposure to unrelated app contentContext and reply options felt too limited

Three issues were prioritised for the second version. Each iteration is traced to participant feedback:

1 · Teach through interaction

Feedback: some animations and configuration explanations were unnecessary.

Version 2 uses an interaction-led onboarding that explains what the mode does, how to adjust it temporarily and what can still notify as usual.

2 · Make activation and saving visible

Feedback: mode switching was not salient, and the saving feedback looked like a button.

Version 2 gives activation a clearer state, adds a prompt to return to the home screen and shows a distinct “Changes Saved” confirmation.

3 · Keep message context without opening the app

Feedback: the restricted reply context pushed users to open the full app.

Version 1 offered “Reply in the App” for multiple messages. Version 2 lets messages unfold in place, so context stays available inside the bounded window.

06Limits and next steps

What the test established, and what comes next

Participants could understand the concept, felt able to control it and could operate the prototype, and their feedback shaped three iterations. Whether Calm Profile reduces notification anxiety or checking, and whether people keep using it, is the question for the next study.

  • Samples are small and from students; the survey is exploratory; the prototype is low-fidelity.
  • What I would carry forward: interaction mapping should extend beyond the moment of interaction; behavioural frameworks help organise mixed-method findings but must be applied cautiously so they do not flatten qualitative complexity.

Next study

A higher-fidelity or in-situ comparison, measuring momentary notification pressure, urge to check, time to return to the task, rate of missed important messages and willingness to keep using it; plus device energy and processing measurements.

Product feasibility assessment (design stage)

The project ends at a tested prototype. What it gives a product team is a defined concept, a rationale for each design choice and a clear list of open questions.
