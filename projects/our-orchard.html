Case study 02 · Team project · Imperial College London

Our Orchard

Many couples share chores unevenly because the planning behind them, the cognitive part, goes unseen. My part was the research that explained why, and the behavioural theory that connected interview evidence to design.

Research questionHow might we facilitate communication to balance cognitive household labour between couples (18–26) who recently moved in together, supporting long-term relationship functioning?
My roleUser research and data analysis; the behavioural theory (COM-B, Self-Determination Theory) behind the design rationale; one member of a team.
Methods and sampleStakeholder insight interviews with six participants; thematic synthesis; COM-B problem analysis; Self-Determination Theory interpretation.
Core findingsHousehold tasks are invisible to the partner; asking for help is itself extra load; partners perceive task difficulty differently.
Impact on decisionsThe interviews supported three design decisions, each traced to a prototype feature. Participants were then shown the design framework and gave qualitative feedback; it led to one change to the prototype, made by the team.

01Context and question

Cognitive household labour is the part of mental load that involves planning tasks, anticipating needs and delegating responsibilities. It is easy to miss because it is not an observable chore. The project asked how a design might help couples bring it into the open.

What research supports here

Choosing what to design: should the product track chores, or make the effort behind them visible and discussable? The interview themes below point to the second.

03Research design

ElementDetail
MethodSemi-structured stakeholder insight interviews with six participants about how tasks are shared and coordinated in close relationships.
Why interviewsThe question concerned invisible, private coordination, which is hard to observe directly, so first-hand accounts were needed to find the mechanisms.
ParticipantsSix people, all of whom had lived with a partner, for less than three years.
AnalysisThematic synthesis; COM-B problem analysis of the target behaviour (imbalanced cognitive household load between cohabiting couples); Self-Determination Theory as a second lens.
ComparatorsExisting task and calendar tools were benchmarked (for example TickTick and TimeTree).
RecruitmentVoluntary participation.
ScaleSix participants, used to find the mechanisms behind the problem and shape the concept. Quotations illustrate themes.
Method detail: COM-B problem analysis

04From interview evidence to design response

Three themes came out of the interviews. Each one is traced from what participants said, through the behavioural theory that explains it, to a specific prototype function. Quotations are verbatim, identified by participant number only.

Choose a theme. The theory explanation is the team’s interpretation of six participants’ interviews; it records why each function exists.

Theme 1 · Tasks are invisible

Household tasks are managed privately, so the other partner has no visible access point.

Interview evidence
“I do almost all of it (chores).” “I do much more of the cat stuff,… that’s one thing I wish he would do more.”P3
“Goes unnoticed by partner and a part of mental loads.”P4
Behavioural theory

COM-B opportunity: tasks are managed privately (mental notes, personal reminders), giving the other partner no visible access point. Self-Determination Theory: autonomy.

Function in the prototype

Visualise chores and contributions: a task grows through Seed, Planting, Flower and Harvest, with easy assignment and shared plans.

Theme 2 · Asking for help adds to the load

Asking for help is itself extra mental load.

Interview evidence
“Communication only comes when there’s a certain level of panic.” “I get overwhelmed.”P3
“Asking for help is a part of the mental load.”P4
Behavioural theory

Self-Determination Theory: relatedness. COM-B motivation: if one partner always takes over, the other has little reason to engage.

Function in the prototype

Light communication and a feedback loop: nudges prompt self-encouragement when you start a task and a reminder to praise your partner when they start.

Theme 3 · Partners perceive task difficulty differently

Partners do not see the same effort in the same task.

Interview evidence
“Hoping he pays it back elsewhere;… expect him to step up in other ways.”P5
Behavioural theory

COM-B: mismatched perceptions of effort can lead to resentment or withdrawal. Self-Determination Theory: relatedness.

Function in the prototype

Visualise different difficulty perceptions: each task is rated for difficulty (1–5), shown as apple size.

05The prototype

A shared task grows through four stages, so each partner’s contribution is visible without asking: Seed (plan the task and rate its difficulty, 1–5) → Planting (start the task; sprouts grow) → Flower (in progress; the apple turns solid) → Harvest (task done). Built-in nudges prompt self-encouragement when you start and a reminder to praise your partner when they start; a calendar shows harvested tasks by day, week, month or year.

06Concept and prototype feedback

Once the prototype took shape, participants were shown the design framework and the thinking behind it, to hear how the concept landed. Not every participant necessarily gave feedback, so I report what was said rather than a count. This was a concept-stage conversation, not a usability test, and I summarise it in my own words rather than as quotations.

What participants said (summary)Where it connects to the design
They liked the apple metaphor and its visual expression of progress.The Seed → Planting → Flower → Harvest growth stages and the apple-size difficulty rating.
They asked for support for low-cost communication between partners.Relates to the theme that asking for help adds to the load. The team responded by adding a low-cost, meme-style way to send encouragement and support (see below).

One confirmed design change

  1. Feedback: a need for low-cost communication between partners.
  2. Team response: a meme-style expression of encouragement and support was added to the prototype.

This was a team decision, not mine alone. I have not tested whether the addition changes how couples communicate or share tasks, so it is a design response, not a validated effect.

How to read this

This is qualitative reaction to a concept. It shows how the metaphor was received and what participants wanted more of; it does not test whether couples can use the app or whether it changes how they share tasks.

07Where the project stands

Stage reached

A working prototype, followed by a concept and prototype-stage review in which participants were shown the design framework and rationale. The interviews gave the design a clear rationale; how the app behaves in couples’ everyday use is the next thing to learn.

Questions for a next round with couples

  • Do couples keep using it after the first weeks?
  • Does it make conversations about tasks easier?
  • Do partners see the split of effort as fairer?
  • Which signals to track: task creation and completion, contribution balance, weekly active couples
